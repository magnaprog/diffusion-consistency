\begin{abstract}
Algebraic constraints restrict possible combinations of variables without determining their conditional probabilities. Solar wind imputation illustrates this distinction through a variance identity linking magnetic statistics. The energy score assesses predictive distributions. When validation selects the model, penalizing the identity residual gives little mean improvement over unpenalized diffusion. At the end of training, the penalty improves the score in each run. Generating the quantities needed for algebraic completion gives the lowest mean score on those quantities. Both representation and training loss change, and coverage remains below nominal. Clipping can enforce the moment constraints yet worsen the score by reducing ensemble spread. An example with an exact denoiser gives small residuals and inaccurate conditional samples. Constraint satisfaction therefore needs separate assessment from predictive accuracy. The evidence concerns hours masked in complete records and a test period examined during earlier development.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Missing solar wind measurements complicate estimates of magnetic turbulence \citep{wrench2022statistics}. Changes in field direction can produce a small mean vector despite large individual magnitudes. Interpolating the mean alone leaves the magnetic fluctuations undetermined. The models infer the joint distribution of six magnetic statistics over a missing hour from measurements before and after it.\footnote{Code and numerical evidence: \nolinkurl{https://github.com/magnaprog/diffusion-consistency}}

A variance identity determines one statistic from the other five, restricting their possible combinations while leaving their conditional probabilities to be learned. It holds for arbitrary vectors, including noise, and is independent of plasma dynamics. The models use conditional diffusion \citep{tashiro2021csdi} with the residual objective and estimator of Physics-Informed Diffusion Models \citep{bastek2025pidm}. Earlier work compares residual minimization with distribution matching \citep{baldan2025physics} and enforces algebraic constraints through output construction \citep{beucler2021constraints}. Comparing a residual penalty with algebraic completion and clipping tests whether satisfying the constraints improves prediction. The penalty's apparent benefit depends on which model state is selected from training.



\section{Magnetic statistics and data}

Let $B_j$ denote five successive OMNI vector averages, each over one minute, from the same spacecraft in geocentric solar ecliptic coordinates. Define
\begin{equation}
 \mu=\frac15\sum_j B_j,\quad m=\frac15\sum_j\|B_j\|,\quad
 v_s=\frac15\sum_j(\|B_j\|-m)^2,\quad
 v_v=\frac15\sum_j\|B_j-\mu\|^2.
\end{equation}
The six variables are $z=(\mu_x,\mu_y,\mu_z,m,v_s,v_v)$. Expanding both population variances gives
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{obs}}(z)=m^2+v_s-\|\mu\|^2-v_v=0.
 \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
Omitting the variances gives $R_{\mathrm{inst}}=m^2-\|\mu\|^2$. A penalty on this quantity suppresses cancellation between field directions. It vanishes when all nonzero vectors share a direction, even if their magnitudes differ.

Write $r=\|\mu\|$. Five vectors in three dimensions, including possible zero vectors, can realize these statistics exactly when
\begin{equation}
 m\ge r,\qquad 0\le v_s\le\frac32m^2+\frac52r^2,\qquad
 v_v=m^2+v_s-r^2.
 \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:support} proves sufficiency. Algebraic completion changes only $v_v$, so the primary score over the other five variables is unchanged. Clipping restricts $m$ and $v_s$ to the ranges in Equation~\ref{eq:support}, then derives $v_v$. Changes to $m$ or $v_s$ can affect the score.

The conditional mean can lie outside the nonlinear set of possible statistics even when every sample obeys the identity. For conditioning information $Y$,
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathbb{E}[z\mid Y])
 =\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\mu\mid Y)-\operatorname{Var}(m\mid Y).
 \label{eq:conditional}
\end{equation}
Either sign is possible. A residual at a denoiser's mean prediction can therefore reflect conditional uncertainty. Residuals at individual samples test whether those samples obey the identity \citep{bastek2025pidm}.

\paragraph{Data construction.} The data come from the modified OMNI high resolution product \citep{nasamodifiedomni,king2005solar}. Timestamps estimate arrival at the bow shock nose; propagation and averaging precede masking \citep{papitashvili2020omni}. The task reconstructs records on this time grid; recovery at the spacecraft's original observation times requires a separate experiment. Magnitudes are Euclidean norms of the vector components. Each window spans four hours in 48 bins of five minutes. The central twelve bins are masked without prior imputation, leaving 90 minutes of conditioning data on each side. Complete windows use one spacecraft within one month, retaining 12.77\% of possible training starts.

In every year from 2005 through 2024, training uses 15 January through 30 June, test uses August and September, and validation uses 1 November through 17 December. The dates between these periods separate the sets. Normalization uses the 300{,}392 distinct bins in 122{,}309 overlapping training windows. Evaluation first packs windows of four hours without overlap, then selects evenly spaced positions within each month up to specified quotas, giving 319 validation and 480 test windows.

\section{Models and evaluation}

\paragraph{Training.} D uses diffusion to generate all six variables \citep{ho2020ddpm}. I-S adds a penalty on $R_{\mathrm{inst}}$; O-S adds one on the variance identity $R_{\mathrm{obs}}$. G5 generates five variables and derives $v_v$ after sampling. All four models receive all six variables in the available intervals. Training and sampling add noise only within the missing hour and preserve the available measurements.

For standardized state $u$ in the missing hour, the training objective adapts \citet{bastek2025pidm} by combining mean squared error with Min-SNR-5 weighting \citep{hang2023minsnr} and a residual term:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_t=w_t\,\operatorname{MSE}(\hat u_0,u_0)
 +\frac{c_R}{2\Sigma_t}\,\frac1{12}\sum_{k=1}^{12}
 \left(\frac{R(z^*_{0,k})}{B_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
Here $\hat u_0$ estimates the signal before noise. Two denoiser evaluations give $z^*_{0,k}$ in physical units for bin $k$, except at $t=1$, where the first estimate is reused. The weight is $w_t=\min(\operatorname{SNR}_t,5)$, with signal-to-noise ratio $\operatorname{SNR}_t$; $B_{\mathrm{ref}}^2$ is the mean of $m^2+v_s$ over distinct training bins. The cosine noise schedule has 100 steps \citep{nichol2021improved}. $\Sigma_t$ is the posterior variance, with $\Sigma_1:=\Sigma_2$. D and G5 use $c_R=0$; I-S and O-S use $c_R=0.0005$.

Each method uses three training seeds and 29{,}636 parameter updates, saving five model states, or checkpoints, per seed (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). Residual estimation uses extra denoiser evaluations, so equal update counts involve different amounts of computation. The comparison uses the original settings and penalty coefficient without further tuning on the test period examined earlier.

\paragraph{Scores and uncertainty.} The energy score evaluates predictive distributions using Euclidean distances \citep{gneiting2007strictly}; lower scores are better. The primary score uses $(\mu_x,\mu_y,\mu_z,m,v_s)$ at twelve hidden positions, giving 60 coordinates per window standardized using training data. For $K$ draws, its unbiased estimate is the mean distance to withheld values minus half the mean distance over the $K(K-1)$ ordered pairs of distinct draws. Scores give equal weight to each month in the evaluation. Other measures are the continuous ranked probability score, coverage and interval width; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives their definitions.
Diffusion results average three training seeds. Coverage is computed separately in each ensemble of 39 draws before averaging. Intervals for score differences come from resampling years, or groups of two consecutive years, with replacement. Each resampling replicate uses the same years for all models and retains the month counts for each occurrence of a year. These intervals describe variation across test years for the existing model fits and draws. Grouping windows by withheld magnitude or increments gives retrospective comparisons of those test cases.

\paragraph{Model selection and baselines.} The 60 diffusion validation ensembles each contain 39 draws, using 25 denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM) evaluations per draw with $\eta=0$ \citep{song2021ddim}. Validation selects checkpoints separately for raw and clipped predictions by minimizing the energy score with equal month weights; exact ties favor the earlier state. Test ensembles contain 39 draws with 50 evaluations each.

Linear interpolation joins the available bins immediately before and after the missing hour; persistence repeats the preceding bin. Gaussian interpolation adds a Gaussian residual vector with 72 coordinates to the interpolant, using a mean and covariance from TRAIN and the original covariance shrinkage $\rho=0$. Gaussian regression predicts the 60 primary residuals from all 216 input coordinates, uses a constant covariance, and derives $v_v$. Validation chooses its ridge regularization and covariance shrinkage parameters before TEST (Appendix~\ref{app:extension}). All comparisons reuse the same baseline ensembles.

\section{Conclusion}

The variance identity constrains combinations of magnetic statistics, leaving their conditional distribution to be learned. The penalty's benefit depends on checkpoint choice, even with equal validation and test sampling effort. Most of G5's mean energy score improvement over D comes from greater ensemble spread. Coverage is below nominal, and variance errors change sign across seeds. Because G5 changes both representation and loss, separating their effects requires further experiments. Distributional scores and coverage must accompany constraint checks and explicit checkpoint selection. The evidence covers hours masked in complete records, three seeds and a test period examined earlier. Naturally missing records, later years, other interval lengths and further training seeds require separate evaluation.

\section{Physically possible magnetic statistics and energy}
\label{app:support}

The realizability conditions follow from the possible sums of five vectors. Let $a_j=\|B_j\|$, so $\sum_j a_j=5m$. Vectors with these lengths can sum to a vector of length $5r$ precisely when $\max(0,2\max_j a_j-5m)\le5r\le5m$. Thus each $a_j$ must be at most $5(m+r)/2$. With the total length fixed, the sum of squared lengths is largest when two lengths are $5(m+r)/2$ and $5(m-r)/2$ and the other three are zero. Hence $m^2+v_s\le5(m^2+r^2)/2$, which gives the upper bound in Equation~\ref{eq:support}.

Equal lengths attain the lower bound $v_s=0$. The nonnegative lengths have a fixed sum and a common upper bound, so their feasible set is convex and contains both extremes. Continuity of the sum of squared lengths then gives every intermediate value. Rotating a nonzero vector sum gives the mean the direction of $\mu$; a zero sum already has the required mean. The conditions are therefore both necessary and sufficient, including when all five vectors vanish.

The quantity $Q=m^2+v_s=\|\mu\|^2+v_v$ is the mean squared magnitude of the five vectors, each already averaged over one minute. Multiplying by $10^{-18}/(2\mu_0)$ converts nT$^2$ to the magnetic energy density represented by those averages. This estimate excludes fluctuations lost in the original minute averages.

Averaging $Q$ over a longer interval requires only the statistics of its constituent bins. The check uses all 97{,}699 eligible intervals of 15 minutes, each starting on a UTC quarter hour within the union of training windows. Combining the three sets of statistics for five minutes agrees with a direct calculation from the original fifteen vectors to a maximum absolute difference of $9.09\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{nT}^2$. Using only the three mean vectors loses $0.8943\,\mathrm{nT}^2$ on average.

\section{Data sources and selection}
\label{app:quality}

Recomputing the magnetic statistics from the original OMNI inputs reproduces their values, normalization and division into training, validation and test sets exactly. The windows also retain their original IDs and order.

The magnetic inputs are the vector components in geocentric solar ecliptic coordinates, GSE, listed as NASA words 15--17. The modified OMNI format identifies Wind as source 52 and ACE as source 71. NASA word 14 contains the average magnitude, which can differ from the magnitude of the averaged vector components used here. Before aggregation over five minutes, data checks reject nonfinite values and fill codes from the OMNI specification. Calendar fields must be integral, timestamps unique and in order, and the spacecraft source common to the whole window. No amplitude cutoff or additional removal of spikes based on the values to be predicted is applied.

NASA estimates arrival times and forms weighted averages for each minute before the OMNI record is masked. Neighboring output minutes can share measurements, and the phase front normals used to estimate propagation depend on neighboring magnetic observations. Any contribution of the hidden interval to those earlier estimates remains after masking. NASA retains arrivals whose order changes after propagation and describes the resulting mixing of plasma regions \citep[Sections 3a--3c and Appendix 5]{papitashvili2020omni}. A common spacecraft source within each window prevents source changes, while arrival order can still differ from observation order. Directional cancellation, variance and changes at gap edges can reflect both this processing and solar wind variability.

Data completeness depends on propagation as well as solar wind conditions. Magnetic observations can be excluded when suitable plasma velocities are missing or when no acceptable phase front normal spans a long interval with little variation. Completeness alone therefore cannot reliably distinguish quiet from disturbed solar wind. NASA selects spacecraft data in segments of three hours using coverage and spatial separation, with a preference for Wind from 2020 onward \citep[Appendix 6]{papitashvili2020omni}. Comparisons between spacecraft groups consequently combine differences in instruments, dates and processing choices. The results use the original OMNI inputs; comparison with NASA's current release remains outstanding.

The training dates contain 4{,}816{,}800 recorded minutes, including 4{,}400{,}682 with valid magnetic vectors. These counts include minutes outside eligible training windows. Among the 4{,}298{,}363 valid magnetic minutes with a known duration between observing times, DBOT, 555{,}719 have negative DBOT, indicating an apparent reversal of observation order at the spacecraft. Of the 300{,}392 distinct eligible bins, 55{,}339 contain at least one such minute. The phase front normal interpolation field gives the percentage of contributions to a magnetic average over one minute whose normal was interpolated \citep{nasamodifiedomni}. This field concerns the propagation normals. These processing measures leave the set of test windows intact.

Of the 957{,}720 possible starts for training windows, 122{,}309 meet the data requirements. The denominator counts starts on the grid of five minutes whose full four hours lie inside a calendar month assigned to training. These windows contain 163{,}988 distinct bins that can occupy a hidden position in at least one window. Availability varies by year: 11{,}340 starts remain in 2005 and 2{,}568 in 2014. The role of solar activity in this missingness is undetermined.

Evaluation limits each month's contribution by first taking eligible windows of four hours in chronological order without overlap within that month. From $N$ such windows, $q=\min(N,q_0)$ are retained at indices $\lfloor(j+0.5)N/q\rfloor$ for $j=0,\ldots,q-1$, with $q_0=8$ for VAL and $q_0=12$ for TEST. The quota limits the influence of months with more complete records, although completeness still determines which windows are eligible.

\section{Model training, sample generation, and scoring}
\label{app:protocol}

The main solar wind comparison has twelve diffusion fits, each trained for 29{,}636 optimizer updates with batches of 128. Each update uses the loss gradient from one batch. Training makes 31 passes through the data, including the shorter batch at the end of each pass.

The U-Net has widths 64/128/128 and contains 1{,}603{,}398 parameters when it generates six coordinates, or 1{,}603{,}013 for G5. The architecture omits attention and dropout. Evaluation uses the fitted weights directly, without exponential averaging. AdamW uses learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, betas $(0.9,0.999)$, epsilon $10^{-8}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and a limit of 1 on the norm of the total gradient.

The model state is saved at 2{,}868, 9{,}560, 16{,}252, 22{,}944, and 29{,}636 optimizer updates. These five checkpoints allow comparisons at common stages of training.

The cosine diffusion schedule has offset 0.008 and an upper limit of 0.999 for beta. Its posterior variance is $\Sigma_t=\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$, with $\Sigma_1:=\Sigma_2$. Observations and score calculations use float64; network calculations and diffusion samples use float32. For evaluation, PCG64 supplies initial noise for six coordinates, of which G5 uses the first five. Training uses a CPU PyTorch generator for timesteps and Gaussian noise in the generated coordinates. The same available measurements are supplied at every denoiser evaluation.

The energy score is also calculated for the vector components alone and for all six coordinates. Other measures include the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), interval coverage and width, identity residuals, failures of the physical inequalities, and errors at gap edges relative to the withheld observations.

G5 predicts five coordinates, so its data loss averages squared error over 60 hidden coordinates; the other diffusion models use 72. Removing the $v_v$ prediction changes both the weight of each remaining coordinate in that average and the network input and output dimensions. This comparison leaves their individual contributions to the score difference unresolved. Algebraic completion alone preserves the primary score when the first five coordinates stay fixed.

Prediction intervals are formed separately for each coordinate from its 39 sorted draws, including both endpoints. Counting from one, draws 2/38 define the 90\% interval and draws 10/30 define the 50\% interval. If the withheld value and ensemble members are independent draws from the same continuous conditional distribution, rank symmetry gives expected coverage $(h-l)/40$ for endpoints $l$ and $h$. Ties in the discrete HMM distribution can raise the coverage of these inclusive intervals.

Errors at gap edges compare the predicted changes between hidden and available bins with the actual changes, separately from changes inside the gap. For interpolation, the mean-vector changes at both edges are $(\mu_{30}-\mu_{17})/13$, which can be nonzero. The Gaussian model also permits nonzero residuals at those edges. The identity residual measures departure from the equality; the fractions in the tables count failures of the physical inequalities.

The mean estimator M evaluates the residual at the first denoiser prediction of the data before noise was added. S makes a DDIM transition from $t$ to 1 without new random noise, then predicts the data again at that lower noise level. Both estimators reverse the affine standardization to return the prediction to physical units before evaluating the residual. At $t=1$, S reuses the first prediction, so M and S coincide. S provides an approximation to a conditional draw.

Gradients of the two loss terms are evaluated on the same batches. For each O-S seed, the calculation uses the same eight TRAIN windows and eight VAL windows at the first and final common checkpoints, with noise seed 7003 and $c_R=0.0005$. Both residuals and both estimators are evaluated at $t\in\{1,25,50,100\}$ while the model weights remain constant. Norms and angles refer to gradients of the weighted data and residual terms before clipping of the total gradient. Undefined ratios and angles remain missing. These measurements characterize the loss gradients on those batches; the cause of differences in test scores remains undetermined.

\subsection{How the residual penalty is weighted}

The relative weight of the residual penalty varies with the diffusion timestep, even at $c_R=0.0005$. With $\lambda_t=c_R/(2\Sigma_t)$, the ratios $\lambda_t/w_t$ at $t=1,25,50,100$ are 0.1239, 0.004183, 0.008642, and 1030.7. At the final noise level, the Min-SNR data weight is only $2.429\times10^{-7}$. These ratios compare loss coefficients; the corresponding gradient ratios also depend on the derivatives of the two terms.

At the final O-S checkpoints, the norm of the residual gradient divided by the norm of the data gradient ranges from 315 to 1799 on TRAIN and 214 to 1266 on VAL at $t=100$. At $t=25$, the ranges are 0.0081 to 0.0267 and 0.0103 to 0.0465. Each range spans three seeds, with one calculation on eight windows per seed. The absolute TRAIN residual-gradient norms at $t=100$ are $7.379\times10^{-5}$ to $4.401\times10^{-4}$, much smaller than the 0.1396 to 0.4587 range at $t=1$. Thus the large ratio at high noise accompanies a small absolute gradient. These measurements precede clipping of the total gradient, and optimizer updates combine noise levels. The residual term's contribution to prediction quality remains unresolved.

\subsection{Choosing checkpoints and prior use of test data}

The solar wind comparison uses new fits because the earlier training records do not identify the code that produced those models. The current implementation also corrects two evaluation errors: the earlier sampler supplied different values outside the gap from those used during training, and the earlier HMM calculation received a switching probability unavailable to the prediction methods. The changed fits prevent a separate estimate of either repair's effect on the earlier scores. The test period had already been examined before this comparison.

D at seed 7 is the only fit for which clipping changes the checkpoint chosen by validation: raw predictions choose 9{,}560 updates, while clipped predictions choose 29{,}636. For D, the mean energy score across the three training seeds is 1.881 when clipping uses the checkpoints chosen from raw predictions. Choosing each checkpoint by its clipped score gives a mean of 1.869.

The original rule for superiority compares raw O-S with four procedures: raw D, raw G5, and D and G5 whose checkpoints are chosen independently for clipping. O-S must have a mean energy score at least two percent lower than each procedure, both difference intervals below zero, and the same direction of difference at all three seeds. A separate rule asks whether the raw O-S versus raw D score difference lies within a prescribed range: both relative intervals must be strictly inside $[-0.02,0.02]$, with positive D score denominators in every resample. Both rules concern score differences; equality of the predictive distributions remains undetermined.

For O-S minus D, resampling groups of two consecutive years gives an interval of $[-0.0345,0.0233]$. The intervals describe temporal variation separately for each comparison, conditional on the existing fits and draws. They omit adjustment for multiple comparisons and variation from new training seeds.

\subsection{Scores during training and the number of sampling steps}
\label{app:checkpoints}

The later checkpoint analysis tests whether doubling the number of denoiser evaluations during validation changes model choice. It covers all five checkpoints for raw D and O-S at each of the three training seeds, using the original weights, windows, 39 draws and evaluation seed 7. The original validation ensembles use 25 denoiser evaluations; 30 new ensembles use 50 with the same initial noise. TEST then covers the same 30 model states at 50 evaluations, reusing ten ensembles after checking their identities and generating twenty more. Figure~\ref{fig:checkpoints} includes all five checkpoints. Validation with 50 evaluations determines the checkpoint choices, and both the choices and evaluation count are fixed before any additional TEST samples are generated. The test period had already been examined in the earlier study.

\begin{samepage}
All six validation choices agree between 25 and 50 evaluations. For these fits and this realization of the initial noise, doubling the validation evaluations leaves the comparison at the chosen checkpoints intact. At the five common update counts, mean TEST differences for O-S minus D are $-0.1238$, $0.0490$, $-0.0470$, $-0.0993$, and $-0.0554$, respectively. O-S has lower scores at all three seeds at the last two counts; seed differences have mixed signs at the first three. Its advantage at the final update is part of this variation during training.

\end{samepage}

D chooses an earlier checkpoint for every seed; O-S chooses the final one for seeds 7 and 13. Replacing these choices with the final checkpoints raises D's mean TEST score by 0.0945 and O-S's by 0.0419, changing their difference from $-0.0028$ to $-0.0554$. D's larger deterioration accounts arithmetically for O-S's larger advantage at the final update. This calculation leaves the roles of regularization and overfitting unresolved.

The years represented in validation can affect which checkpoint is chosen. In 10{,}000 resamples of the twenty years, D at seed 7 chooses update 9{,}560 in 61.0\% of resamples with 25 evaluations and 71.5\% with 50; all other resamples choose the final checkpoint. D at seeds 13 and 42 retains its respective earlier choice in every resample under either evaluation count. Each occurrence of a sampled year contributes both validation months, and every checkpoint uses the same resampled years. The model weights and draws remain constant, so these frequencies measure variation across validation years. Variation under fresh sampling noise and performance on a new population remain undetermined. The numerical results include ranks, score margins, resampling frequencies and choices after omitting each year in turn, for both methods and both evaluation counts.

\label{app:oracle}

For $\alpha\in\{0,1,2\}$, let $c_j=j-3$ for $j=1,\ldots,5$ and $d_\alpha=\sqrt{1+2\alpha^2}$. The two sets of magnetic statistics, $z_0$ and $z_1$, follow from
\begin{equation}
 B_j^{(0)}=\frac{(1,\alpha c_j,0)}{d_\alpha},\qquad
 B_j^{(1)}=\frac{2(1+2\alpha c_j,\,2-2\alpha c_j,\,2+\alpha c_j)}{3d_\alpha}.
 \label{eq:toystates}
\end{equation}
Each state has the same mean squared magnitude across geometries: $Q_0=1$ and $Q_1=4$ in HMM units for every $\alpha$. Changing $\alpha$ alters the vector geometry and magnitude variance inside each bin while preserving these two energies and their difference. Standardization gives equal weight to all six states, with $B_{\mathrm{ref}}^2=2.5$, and is determined before evaluation.

The latent sequence is a two-state chain with switching probability $p\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5\}$ and transition matrix $T_p=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1-p&p\\p&1-p\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. Its endpoint states follow the stationary law $\Pr(a)=1/2$ and $\Pr(b\mid a,p)=(T_p^{13})_{ab}$, and the twelve hidden states are drawn conditional on those endpoints.

In the record of four hours and 48 bins, the 18 bins before the gap repeat the left state's statistics and the 18 bins after it repeat the right state's statistics. These available intervals encode $\alpha$ and the endpoint states, leaving $p$ unknown to the network. A Markov trajectory connects the endpoints through the twelve hidden states.

The exact conditional calculation marginalizes over the three switching probabilities, assigned equal prior weights. Before noisy evidence is included, the weights conditional on the endpoints are proportional to $\Pr(p)(T_p^{13})_{ab}$ for the stationary construction and to $\Pr(p)$ for the earlier design with independent endpoint states. At every denoiser evaluation, the current noisy input further updates these weights.

Writing $\kappa_\alpha=[1+2\sqrt{1+\alpha^2}+2\sqrt{1+4\alpha^2}]/5$, the residual for posterior probability $q$ of state 1 is:
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigl((1-q)z_0+qz_1\bigr)
 =q(1-q)\frac{11/3-\kappa_\alpha^2}{1+2\alpha^2}.
 \label{eq:toymean}
\end{equation}
The coefficient is positive for $\alpha=0,1$ and negative for $\alpha=2$. Thus a nondegenerate mixture has a nonzero residual in all three geometries, even though each individual state obeys the identity. Squaring the residual assigns a positive penalty to the exact posterior mean whenever both states have positive posterior probability. This magnetic example illustrates the effect of taking expectations through a nonlinear residual discussed by \citet{bastek2025pidm}.

The learned HMM comparison uses 4{,}096 training windows generated at seed 106 by cycling through the nine $(\alpha,p)$ combinations. The U-Net has widths 32/64/64, batches contain 128 windows, and each fit receives 5{,}000 optimizer updates at seed 7. Other optimizer and diffusion settings follow Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}. Evaluation uses 32 paths per switching probability, reusing each path for the three values of $\alpha$. The resulting 288 windows thus share hidden state sequences across vector geometries.

Table~\ref{tab:oracle} compares methods on the same 288 windows with stationary endpoint states, the same available information and 39 draws per method. The approximate samplers share their initial noise. The first row compares independent draws from the exact conditional distribution with the hidden values. It estimates the minimum expected score under that law, subject to Monte Carlo error. A finite estimate can lie above or below this expectation. Earlier windows with independent endpoint states are part of a separate sensitivity analysis and are excluded here.

\subsection{Interval coverage, width, and score contributions}

In Table~\ref{tab:calibration}, O-S's absolute departure from nominal 90\% coverage exceeds D's by 2.8 percentage points, beyond the prespecified two-point threshold. Gaussian interpolation has coverage near nominal for its 90\% intervals but substantial overcoverage for its 50\% intervals. Calibration conditional on the available measurements requires further assessment.

Table~\ref{tab:channelcoverage} gives coverage by channel: O-S covers scalar variance more often than D, but covers each vector component and mean magnitude less often. Similar primary energy scores can conceal these differences because the score evaluates a distribution over all 60 coordinates, whereas coverage checks each coordinate and position separately.

At the checkpoints chosen using raw validation draws, G5 has a slightly smaller mean distance to the withheld measurements than D and a larger spread term. The score subtracts the spread term, whose increase accounts for most of the mean improvement in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}(a). Both terms use the same 60 standardized coordinates and equal month and seed weights.

The two score terms change differently across seeds. At seed 13, G5 decreases both distance to the measurements and spread, with the larger decrease in distance. At seed 42, both increase, with the larger increase in spread. Both patterns lower the score. This decomposition explains the score differences for the existing predictions. It cannot identify a training mechanism or separate G5's changes to representation and loss.

\paragraph{Where observations fall outside the intervals.}
G5's nominal 90\% intervals still miss many observations, especially for $v_s$, whose coverage is 40.5\%. To distinguish the direction of these errors, the tail calculation counts observations strictly below the second sorted draw, inside the inclusive interval, and strictly above the 38th. If the observation and all 39 ensemble members are independent draws from the same continuous conditional distribution, their exchangeable ranks imply expected fractions of 5\%, 90\%, and 5\%. A fitted model's coverage must be measured.

Every TEST window contributes to the calculation. For each model, the calculation averages positions within a window and windows within a month, then weights months and models equally. Coverage checks for individual variables complement the proper score \citep{gneiting2007calibration} and can expose errors that an overall coverage percentage can hide.

Averaged over the three fits, 49.0\% of observations lie below G5's $v_s$ intervals and 10.5\% above them. The seed dots in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} show a reversal that the averages obscure: errors lie predominantly below the intervals at seeds 7 and 13, and above them at seed 42. D also reverses direction at seed 42. O-S's mean fractions are 37.7\% below and 8.4\% above. Wider intervals still leave many observations uncovered.

For G5's $v_s$ intervals, the fraction below exceeds the fraction above by 38.4 percentage points, with a 95\% interval of $[36.2,40.6]$ from resampling years. The difference is calculated before rounding the two tail percentages. Each of 10{,}000 resamples calculates both fractions on the same sampled years and retains every occurrence of a repeated year. The interval concerns this one contrast, conditional on the existing models, checkpoints and draws; it excludes variation from new training seeds or a new checkpoint choice.

Gaussian regression covers 98.1\% of $v_s$ observations, with 0.6\% below and 1.2\% above its intervals. Its wide intervals accompany a worse primary energy score. Coverage, interval width and score describe different properties of the predictive distribution and should be read together. Both temporal resampling schemes include every method and coordinate.

\subsection{Vector increments at the edges and inside the missing interval}

The increments at each gap edge measure the change between available and generated vectors: indices 17 to 18 at the left edge and 29 to 30 at the right. Table~\ref{tab:geometry} reports their mean magnitudes and the error of individual draws. RMSE is calculated across the 39 draws within each window, then averaged over months and seeds. Because this RMSE depends on both predictive spread and bias, a smaller value alone does not establish a better conditional distribution. The error of the ensemble mean is a different quantity.

The withheld record's mean increment magnitudes are 1.0460 nT at the left edge, 1.0539 nT at the right edge, and 1.0831 nT inside the missing hour. Interpolation produces smooth increments by construction. Recovery of missing transients must be assessed against the withheld measurements; smoothness alone is insufficient.

The variogram score checks differences between coordinates, with exponent $1/2$ \citep{scheuerer2015variogram}. Here it averages equally over 175 pairs of standardized primary coordinates: 55 pairs at adjacent times within a channel and 120 pairs between channels at the same time. For pair $(i,j)$, define $d_k=|x_{k,i}-x_{k,j}|^{1/2}$ for ensemble member $k$. Its contribution is $(|y_i-y_j|^{1/2}-\bar d)^2-s_d^2/K$, where $K=39$ and $s_d^2$ uses divisor $K-1$. The squared term compares the observed coordinate difference with its ensemble estimate. For independent draws, the variance of that estimate introduces upward Monte Carlo bias; subtracting $s_d^2/K$ removes this bias in expectation. The final score is the mean contribution over the 175 pairs.

\subsection{Directional cancellation with thresholds from TRAIN}

Directional cancellation occurs when the norm of the average vector is smaller than the average of the individual vector magnitudes. The cancellation analysis asks whether the identity penalty behaves differently in such cases. For each bin, define $C=(m^2-\|\mu\|^2)/m^2$, with $C=0$ when $m=0$, and $A=(m^2-\|\mu\|^2)/B_{\mathrm{ref}}^2$. Both are dimensionless. $C$ measures the difference relative to that bin's squared mean magnitude; $A$ instead uses the fixed TRAIN reference scale.

Group membership uses mean $C$ over the 36 available bins or mean $A$ over the twelve hidden bins. High cancellation means strictly above the TRAIN 75th percentile: 0.044395 for the available intervals and 0.022696 for the missing interval. All 122{,}309 overlapping training windows contribute equally to these thresholds; normalization instead uses distinct bins. Although the rules were specified earlier, they were implemented after inspection of the primary results, so the analysis remains exploratory.

The energy groups use window means of $Q/B_{\mathrm{ref}}^2$. Here $Q=m^2+v_s$ is a magnetic second moment in nT$^2$; the ratio is dimensionless and is a different quantity from the statistical energy score. TRAIN quartile thresholds are $(0.349809,0.571606,1.078729)$ for the available intervals and $(0.343518,0.563106,1.069013)$ for the missing interval. A tie enters the higher energy group, whereas a tie at the cancellation threshold enters the rest group.

The analysis covers all 21 groups: the full test set, high cancellation and the rest, and combinations of cancellation with energy conditions. Group membership and scores for all 18 procedures are recorded. Each score gives equal weight to the months present, then averages the three diffusion seeds.

High cancellation in the available measurements identifies 128 windows across all twenty years. O-S minus D is $-0.0256$, with a 95\% interval of $[-0.0740,0.0147]$ from resampling years. All three seed differences are negative, but the interval still includes zero. O-S minus G5 is $0.0573$, with interval $[0.0053,0.1246]$, while O-S minus D is $0.0114$ in the remaining 352 windows. These results do not establish an O-S advantage over both methods that learn from data alone. \subsection{Algebraic completion and clipping of the existing draws}

Two operations are applied separately to the same ensembles from the checkpoints selected by raw validation score. Model weights, draws and windows remain fixed. Algebraic completion derives $v_v$ from the other five coordinates, leaving those coordinates and their primary energy score exactly unchanged. Training G5 also changes the generated representation and the loss, so its comparison includes more than algebraic completion alone.

Clipping can change the primary coordinates. The map retains $\mu$, raises $m$ to $r=\|\mu\|$ when necessary, and clips $v_s$ to $[0,1.5m^2+2.5r^2]$ using the updated $m$, before deriving $v_v$. The result satisfies the moment constraints and is realizable. Reapplying the map preserves it to the stated arithmetic tolerance. Minimum displacement is not established for this map.

For primary draws $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_K)$ and observation $y$, the empirical energy score obeys the displacement bound
\begin{equation}
 \left|\operatorname{ES}(CX,y)-\operatorname{ES}(X,y)\right|
 \le \frac{2}{K}\sum_{i=1}^K\|Cx_i-x_i\|.
 \label{eq:movement}
\end{equation}
The reverse triangle inequality bounds the change in each score term by the mean displacement of the draws. Adding the two bounds gives Equation~\ref{eq:movement}, without requiring a convex realizable set. The bound limits the magnitude of the score change; either sign is possible. Distances use all 60 primary coordinates after TRAIN standardization and are dimensionless. In Table~\ref{tab:operators}, the change in distance to the withheld measurements is added and the change in the spread term is subtracted.

\subsection{Multivariate Gaussian regression}

Gaussian regression predicts departures from interpolation using all 216 standardized available coordinates on both sides of the missing hour. Let $X$ contain these coordinates and $Y$ the 60 primary residuals from interpolation between bins 17 and 30, divided by the corresponding TRAIN scales. Fitting $W$ and an unpenalized intercept $b$ on TRAIN minimizes $\|Y-XW-b\|_F^2/N+\lambda\|W\|_F^2$, with equal weight for all 122{,}309 overlapping windows.

The nine candidates combine $\lambda\in\{0.001,0.1,1\}$ with covariance $(1-\rho)S+\rho\operatorname{diag}(S)+10^{-6}I$, where $\rho\in\{0,0.5,1\}$ and $S$ is the empirical residual covariance with divisor $N$. The conditional mean changes with context, while this residual covariance stays fixed. The Gaussian law covers the first five coordinates; deriving $v_v$ produces a non-Gaussian distribution over all six. A positive-definite covariance defines a valid Gaussian law but does not enforce the moment constraints on its draws.

For each VAL window, all candidates use the same 39 innovations, generated with PCG64 and identified by split, window, draw and seed 13. Validation minimizes energy score with equal month weights, separately for raw and clipped draws, resolving exact ties in lexicographic $(\lambda,\rho)$ order. Both procedures select $\lambda=0.1$ and $\rho=0$, with VAL scores 1.73275 and 1.83453. These choices were recorded before generating the single TEST ensemble.

Relative to Gaussian interpolation, regression changes the conditional mean fit and the finite noise realization as well as using more available measurements. Its score difference cannot isolate the benefit of the additional context alone.

G5 minus Gaussian regression has a mean difference in energy score of $-0.1503$, with year interval $[-0.2302,-0.0829]$ and interval from resampling groups of two years $[-0.2105,-0.0913]$. For O-S, the mean difference is $-0.0502$, with intervals $[-0.1336,0.0327]$ and $[-0.0913,-0.0080]$, respectively. G5's intervals are negative under both schemes. O-S's interval includes zero when resampling years and excludes zero when resampling groups of two years. The grouping changes the resampling distribution and the uncertainty in this contrast.

These marginal 95\% intervals use the original 10{,}000 temporal resamples and the same sampled years for both methods in each contrast. They hold the three diffusion fits and one Gaussian ensemble fixed and exclude variation from new fits, seed resampling and the validation choice. The later comparison uses a TEST period examined earlier.

Before clipping, Gaussian regression's vector energy score is 1.5728 in 36 standardized coordinates. Its CRPS for mean resolved magnetic energy density is $1.0497\times10^{-12}$ J\,m$^{-3}$. These measure errors in different predictions on different scales. Clipping preserves the vector predictions but raises the CRPS for energy density to $2.1873\times10^{-12}$ J\,m$^{-3}$. Mean displacement over the 60 standardized primary coordinates is 1.7733.

As with Gaussian interpolation, clipping Gaussian regression reduces the spread term more than the mean distance to the withheld measurements. Subtracting this smaller spread term raises the primary energy score even though the draws satisfy the moment constraints. Aggregate coverage near nominal and physical realizability do not by themselves establish an accurate predictive distribution.

\section{Related work on physical constraints in prediction and generation}

Gaussian process models estimate the solar wind at Mars between intermittent observations and quantify uncertainty \citep{azari2024virtual}. Feedforward networks estimate second-order structure functions from incomplete magnetic records \citep{wrench2022statistics}. Diffusion models also predict the evolution of solar coronal images, with images as both conditioning data and predictions \citep{francisco2024corona}. The solar wind experiment here models the joint distribution of magnetic statistics throughout a missing hour, conditional on measurements on both sides.

\citet[Section 3.3 and Appendix A.6.1]{bastek2025pidm} distinguish a residual at the denoiser mean from a residual at a sample, and demonstrate collapse when training uses the residual alone. The HMM here uses two evaluations of an exact denoiser to examine the sampling approximation separately from errors in learning the denoiser. The solar wind experiments compare stages of training and apply algebraic completion or clipping to the same samples. They assess effects on the predictive score that the residual alone cannot determine.

Physical emulators that produce one prediction for each input also use algebraic completion. \citet{beucler2021constraints} compare training on all outputs, including those from conservation layers, with training only on direct outputs and completing the rest algebraically. G5 uses the latter construction in a conditional generator, changing both the generated representation and the training loss. Deriving $v_v$ after sampling leaves the primary score unchanged. In climate downscaling, exact aggregation and nonnegativity impose distinct constraints, and predictive accuracy requires separate assessment \citep{harder2023hard}.

PGDM downscales land surface temperature and compares arithmetic averaging with the fourth root of the mean fourth power of temperature \citep{zhang2025pgdm}. The latter rule neglects variation in emissivity. PGDM conditions on geophysical variables, trains with MSE, and measures error in physical consistency during evaluation. WIND uses diffusion forcing for pretraining and moment matching for posterior guidance to reconstruct weather trajectories from observations that include spatial and temporal averages \citep{aich2026wind}. For guidance, the clean state conditional on the noisy state is approximated by a Gaussian. Its covariance is then propagated through a local linearization of the observation operator. Both methods place aggregation in the observation model, with training and inference that differ from the residual penalty examined here.

PILD models residuals with a Laplace likelihood whose scale adapts to their magnitude. For conditional tasks, it aligns representations with the output of an encoder whose weights remain fixed during diffusion training \citep{zeng2026pild}. To adjust the scale, it approximates conditional moments of residual magnitude from minibatch statistics with exponential averaging. Inference uses two denoiser evaluations, compared with 50 here. Physics-Based Flow Matching integrates the flow to $t=1$ before evaluating residuals and combines gradients of the flow matching and residual losses through ConFIG \citep{baldan2025physics}. The present experiment keeps $c_R$ constant throughout training; adaptive objectives and gradient combinations fall outside its scope.
